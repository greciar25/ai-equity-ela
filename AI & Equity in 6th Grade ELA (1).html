AI & Equity in 6th Grade ELA

An exploration of how AI recommends “appropriate” middle-school texts—and how those recommendations can reproduce patterns around familiarity, culture, geography, and student identity.

Specific Focus
Appropriate texts for 6th graders

Investigating how AI defines, recommends, and contextualizes books for students.

Equity issues AI might replicate

What patterns could show up?

  • Recommendations may center well-known authors and books instead of a wider range of cultural and linguistic perspectives.
  • AI may repeatedly suggest middle-school “classics” because they are highly represented and familiar.
  • Student interests, identities, and lived experiences may be overlooked.
  • “Appropriate” is subjective and depends on maturity, reading level, needs, and context.
Ways to investigate

Test the pattern

  1. Ask the same question multiple times and compare titles.
  2. Change the student population or geographic context.
  3. Vary reading levels and genres.
  4. Compare different AI tools.
  5. Track what is added, dropped, repeated, or assumed.
Brainstorming Chat: NA — the exploration was grounded in the teacher’s own prior knowledge and classroom experience.

Finding 1
Repetition & familiarity

AI returns to the “usual” books

Pattern: Across multiple interactions, AI repeatedly returned to a familiar foundation of books that are commonly associated with sixth grade.

Evidence: Explorations #1, #2, and #3 repeatedly surfaced titles such as Hatchet, Wonder, Holes, and The Giver. Even after repeated prompting, only a small number of titles changed.

Why it matters: Familiar texts can provide a strong benchmark for grade-level expectations and literary analysis. However, relying too heavily on the same canon can narrow the range of authors, experiences, and perspectives students encounter.

Finding 2
Geography & identity

AI can turn location into identity

Pattern: When the prompt named a student’s location, AI changed the genres, themes, and cultural assumptions attached to that student.

Evidence: London prompts produced titles such as Harry Potter and Charlie and the Chocolate Factory. Brooklyn prompts leaned toward race, history, urban life, and athletic goals. El Paso prompts leaned toward immigration, family, work ethic, and language. Explorations #4–#8 also showed demographic and physical characteristics changing with the location.

Why it matters: Geography can inform context, but it does not define a student’s identity. A student who lives in New York, for example, may have any racial, cultural, linguistic, or personal background. AI recommendations can become misleading when place is treated as a shortcut for identity.

Key takeaway

Use AI as a collaborator—not the curriculum gatekeeper.

AI can quickly generate useful starting points, but an ELA teacher still needs to evaluate representation, reading level, maturity, student interests, cultural relevance, instructional purpose, and the assumptions embedded in a recommendation.
